Redbelly grew out of a decade of consensus research, and the papers are the reason some of this network's properties are stronger than the usual EVM assumptions. You can build without reading them, but you do need to know what each one guarantees and where the guarantee stops. I have read every paper on Redbelly's [research page](https://redbelly.network/research) and both company documents, twenty-three in all as of 23 September 2026, and this page is what they come to for your code. Nothing here carries a throughput or latency figure from a paper, on purpose: every one of those describes a research system on rented machines, and the numbers that describe the live network are on the [status page](/status/) and the pages that measured them.

## The four that decide what you delete

**DBFT: Efficient Leaderless Byzantine Consensus and its Application to Blockchains.** Crain, Gramoli, Larrea, Raynal, IEEE NCA 2018. Most Byzantine consensus protocols pick a leader who proposes the next block; if the leader is slow or malicious, everyone waits or votes them out. DBFT has no leader. Every node proposes, the protocol reduces "which proposals get in" to many parallel yes-or-no decisions, and a weak coordinator helps them converge without being able to block them. Decisions are deterministic once made, so all correct nodes agree on one block and there is no fork to resolve later, as long as fewer than a third of them are faulty. Its proofs and its measurements are in a companion technical report rather than in the paper, which matters when someone cites it for a number.

For your code: [consensus and finality](/concepts/consensus-and-finality/). A receipt is final; delete the confirmation-count waiting.

**Red Belly: A Secure, Fair and Scalable Open Blockchain.** Crain, Natoli, Gramoli, IEEE S&P 2021. The paper behind the super block. Instead of one proposer's block, the block that gets committed is the union of proposals from many nodes, which is why a Redbelly block can contain transactions from every governor. It also shares the work of verifying signatures across nodes, and it argues fairness: a transaction that a correct sender submits is eventually included, provided the sender does not submit conflicting transactions and the network is not flooded. That is a promise of inclusion, not of order. Its chain is a UTXO payment system with no EVM. The ordering rule it documents is the one Redbelly told me on 17 September 2026 changes in Q4 2026.

**Smart Redbelly Blockchain: Reducing Congestion for Web3.** Tennakoon, Hua, Gramoli, IEEE IPDPS 2023, with a longer arXiv version. This is the chain this site rests on: DBFT under an EVM derived from geth, with blocks produced when there are transactions. Its central change is what it calls validation and propagation reduction: a transaction is validated once, by the node you sent it to, and then travels only inside proposed blocks; nodes do not pass transactions to each other. The consequence the authors state themselves is that a transaction sent only to a node that declines it is not included, and the remedy is for the client to resend when no receipt arrives. Whether the deployed network keeps this exactly is the first question I put to Redbelly on 22 September 2026 (question 42); their whitepaper says the SEVM does this validation reduction, and their yellow paper says a wallet may have to resend.

For your code: [blocks on demand](/concepts/blocks-on-demand/) has the polling and the resend rule.

**Holistic Verification of Blockchain Consensus.** Bertrand, Gramoli, Konnov, Lazić, Tholoniat, Widder, DISC 2022. People say "formally verified" loosely, and this paper is exact about it. What was checked by machine, with the ByMC parameterised model checker, is the binary consensus at the heart of DBFT, as pseudocode, for any number of nodes with fewer than a third faulty: safety without conditions, and liveness under a fairness assumption that replaces the timers. What was not checked is everything around it: the reduction from many proposals to one super block, the node software that runs the algorithm, the EVM, and your contract.

For your code: do not let "verified consensus" leak into "verified application". Your contract still needs tests in all five credential states, static analysis, and for anything holding value an audit. [Review before mainnet](/review/) lists them.

## The two the company wrote

**The yellow paper, version 1.0.** Redbelly Network, file dated November 2024. It is the company's 2022 architecture article, *Redbelly Blockchain: a Combination of Recent Advances* (Bulletin of the EATCS 137), republished word for word under a cover, and it is the company's standing technical description until the next version, which is where the ordering change is due to be written down. It states the things the research papers leave to inference: the network has consensus nodes and separate SEVM nodes, because consensus needs more than three times the faulty count and correct data retrieval only twice; a transaction is final the moment it is in a block; the wallet Redbelly describes sends to one node and resends if need be, and reads by fetching f + 1 identical copies of state from several SEVM nodes; governors are elected by the sitting governors under a Byzantine-tolerant single transferable vote; reconfiguration and software upgrades are built-in contract functions invoked by a quorum of governors; and the governance holds a built-in rewrite function that, on the vote of a quorum, erases a suffix of the chain at every honest SEVM node. That last one is the reason I never write "immutable" on this site without saying against whom. Whether the rewrite function is live on the deployed network is question 54; ordering inside a super block, "in the order of their hash or nonce" in this version, is question 5.

**The whitepaper.** *Redbelly Network: An accountable data layer for AI powered financial services*, 28 August 2025. The product described by the company, in marketing register and unreviewed, which makes it the right source for what Redbelly says the network is and the wrong source for any number. It confirms that the SEVM carries the validation reduction above, names two staked node roles, and names parts of the platform that this site does not cover because I have not seen them deployed: a Policy SDK, a First Party Oracle SDK, an MEV mitigation by commit-reveal, and bridged BTC (questions 47 to 49). Its throughput sentence cites the 2018 DBFT paper, which contains no measurements, so I do not repeat it.

## Past a third: what accountability is, and is not

**Polygraph: Accountable Byzantine Agreement.** Civit, Gilbert, Gramoli, ICDCS 2021, with a brief at DISC 2020. Consensus is safe while fewer than a third of nodes misbehave. Polygraph asks what happens past that line, and its answer is accountability: if enough nodes collude to cause a disagreement, the correct nodes end up holding signed, irrefutable proof naming at least a third of the guilty. It does not stop the disagreement; it makes it attributable. The proof is exchanged among the nodes running consensus, not handed to clients, so a dApp is not given a certificate to check (question 50).

**ZLB: A Blockchain to Tolerate Colluding Majorities.** Ranchal-Pedrosa and Gramoli, DSN 2024. This is what "tolerates a colluding majority" is built from, and the phrase is easy to misread. Above a third faulty the chain does fork; ZLB then uses Polygraph's proofs to vote the guilty out, votes candidates in from a pool, merges the branches and recomputes state, and in its payment design funds any double spend from the deposits of the guilty. The paper's own words are that a transaction in that design should not be treated as final until it is a number of blocks deep, and that a contract's returned state can be reverted with a reimbursement. So there are two regimes: below a third, no fork, proven; above it, a recovery that is stronger than any chain without accountability offers but is a recovery. The yellow paper carries both (a transaction "is either pending or committed", and the network "can recover" from more than a third faulty) without saying which the deployed network runs (question 56).

**ComChain: A Blockchain with Byzantine Fault Tolerant Reconfiguration.** Vizier and Gramoli, Concurrency and Computation: Practice and Experience, 2020. How the set of deciders changes without stopping: the current set decides the next in a configuration block, signed by enough of the current members. Clients learn the current set from a directory service assumed correct. Together with ZLB's fraud-driven exclusion, this is the likely shape of how governors change on the deployed network (question 44).

For your code: a dApp can assume that a consensus failure would be forensic rather than silent, and that recovery is designed for; it cannot assume that it can never happen, or that state can never move under it in that case. A chain that holds real assets still wants a pause and an admin behind a Safe. The [review page](/review/) has the threat model.

## Ordering, and what "MEV mitigation" cites

**Aion** (Zarbafian and Gramoli, ESORICS 2023), **fair separability** (ESORICS 2024), **Lyra** (IPDPS 2023) and **AOAB** (Gramoli, Lu, Tang, Zarbafian, DSN 2024) are four designs for ordering transactions fairly, by send time with trusted hardware clocks, by the median of arrival positions, and by commit-reveal that hides a transaction's content until it is committed. The whitepaper cites Lyra and AOAB for its MEV mitigation, and two limits go with that. Aion says in its own words that fair ordering alone does not prevent front-running without a commit-reveal step. AOAB's ordering guarantee applies only between transactions that do not arrive close together; two that do may be output in either order. None of the four is confirmed as deployed (question 47).

For your code: do not build on order within a block, and do not expect a priority fee to buy position, because the measured priority fee is zero on both networks. The [consensus page](/concepts/consensus-and-finality/) has the rule as it stands.

## The one paper that tested the shipped node

**Stabl.** Middleware 2025. The only published work that ran Redbelly's own node software (version 0.36.2) rather than a research prototype, on a small cluster, against crashes, partitions and a rejoining node. It tolerated the crashes it was designed to tolerate and recovered from a partition, with the recovery time set by a reconnect timeout the developers confirmed could be shortened. Two sentences in it matter more than its measurements. Trusting a single node, the authors write, brings the number of tolerated Byzantine faults to zero, and Redbelly's recommended client for that reason is a library, credence.js, that cross-checks its reads across several nodes. And sending each transaction to several nodes made Redbelly faster, because a transaction that reaches several proposers is included a little earlier.

For your code: I have not found credence.js published anywhere yet (question 53), and the ops kit's [RPC helper](/operate/) fails over between endpoints, which is a different guarantee from cross-checking them. Until the kit does that, treat two endpoints that answer differently as a reason to stop.

## Methods, context, and one mechanic worth keeping

**Diablo** (EuroSys 2023) is the founder's group's benchmark of six chains under real application workloads, and Redbelly is not among them. It is here for two reasons: it is the argument against unconditioned throughput claims, which is the rule this site follows, and it records that the geth-derived EVM has no hard per-transaction gas cap where three other chains do, a property Redbelly confirmed the deployed network keeps on 12 October 2026 (question 43). **Bare metal evaluations** (SDLT 2023) is a methods paper whose one rule I have adopted for our own numbers: report distributions, not averages. **Planetary Scale Byzantine Consensus** (invited paper, 2023) explains why the super block scales with the number of proposers. **From Blockchain Consensus Back to Byzantine Consensus** (2020) is the founder's case for a network of known participants, which is the reasoning behind the identity gate on this network, in his framing rather than mine. **Anonymity-preserving vector consensus** (ESORICS 2020) contributes a mechanic rather than a claim: traceable ring signatures over a known set give one vote per member with the voter hidden, which is the shape of a shareholder or governance vote on a gated token. The **OPODIS 2023 invited talk** is the best single narrative of how the papers became the product.

**Project Acacia, final report.** Reserve Bank of Australia and the Digital Finance CRC, 18 May 2026. Not a Redbelly paper, but the first primary source that places the network in a regulated setting: Redbelly was one of three platforms on which the RBA issued pilot wholesale CBDC to participants. Pilots, not production, and the report assesses no platform. The [idea pool](/tutorials/) says what it supports and no more.

## What none of them covers

Your contract. Every proof above stops at the consensus algorithm as pseudocode, and every measurement stops at a research system or a small test cluster. The five credential states, static analysis, an audit for anything that holds value, and a rehearsed pause are still yours, and [review before mainnet](/review/) is the list.

## The questions this site cites

- **Question 7.** Whether deploying a contract needs anything beyond a verified wallet.

- **Question 23.** What conduct leads to a credential being revoked, and whether there is notice, appeal or reinstatement.

- **Question 29.** Which release of the Iden3 contracts Redbelly's verifier backend deploys from.
- **Question 30.** Why Anvil's public default accounts 0 and 1 pass the network's access check.
- **Question 38.** How two contract calls that touch the same storage are ordered inside a super block.
- **Question 42.** Answered by Redbelly on 12 October 2026: the validation-once design is deployed, and the RPC endpoint resends a transaction to another governor after 30 seconds ([blocks on demand](/concepts/blocks-on-demand/)).

- **Question 50.** Whether a client can read the consensus certificates or proofs of fraud, to verify finality itself.
- **Question 53.** What credence.js is, where it is published, and whether Redbelly still recommends it.
- **Question 54.** Whether the governance history-rewrite function in the yellow paper is live on the deployed network.
- **Question 56.** Which of ZLB's recovery phases the deployed network runs, and whether a finalization depth exists.
